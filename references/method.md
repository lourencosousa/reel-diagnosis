# Method

Work through these steps in order. Do the thinking here; the report only shows the conclusions.

## 1. Verdict

1. List the baseline reels: plays, and likes and comments where known. Leave out pinned reels and reels younger than 3 days.
2. Work out the median plays and the median likes per play.
3. Place this reel: plays as a multiple of the median (for example 1.4x), and likes per play as a multiple of the median.
4. Name its pattern from `signals.md`: breakout, reached but did not land, loved but did not travel, missed, or in line.
5. **Small numbers.** If the baseline reels average fewer than 20 likes, rates per play are noise: one like moves them. Judge the verdict on plays alone, name the pattern as "missed", "in line" or "breakout" on reach only, and say in one line that the account is too small for likes to mean anything. Never call a reel "loved" on a handful of likes.
6. **Look at the dates before the video.** Sort the baseline by date. If this reel sits inside a run of reels that all did the same (a stretch of lows or highs that starts and stops on a date), the cause is probably outside the video: distribution, posting time, cross posting, a change on the account. Say so first. Then use the video only to explain the gap between this reel and its neighbours.
7. With fewer than 5 baseline reels, say the baseline is thin. If the account posts mostly carousels or photos, say so too: its reels have less of a track record, and its followers may be used to another format.
8. Check what bends the numbers: paid partnership, trending audio, age, hidden likes, collaboration. If one applies, the verdict says so.

With Insights, go further: skip rate against the typical skip rate, average watch time as a share of the length, shares per view and the share of views from non-followers. These are the real signals; plays are only the result.

## 2. Symptoms

From the verdict, name the one or two signals that explain the result. Examples:

- Plays high, likes per play low: the reel was shown widely and did not land. Look at the payoff, and at whether it was boosted.
- Plays low, likes per play high: those who saw it liked it, but Instagram did not push it. Look at the opening and at whether anything in it is worth sending.
- Skip rate above typical: the first 3 seconds failed. Nothing later explains the result as much.
- A steep drop at one second in the retention graph: something happens, or stops happening, at that second.

## 3. Causes

Go to the moments that drive those signals and find what caused them.

**Opening (0 to 3 seconds), drives skip rate.** Look at `hook.jpg`. Answer:
- What does a stranger understand in the first second, with the sound off?
- Is there a question, a tension or a promise? Or only a mood?
- When does the subject (the person, the object, the idea) first appear?
- Does the first line of text or voice give a reason to stay?

**Middle, drives watch time.** Look at the overview sheets, the cut list, the pauses.
- Does each beat add something, or repeat the last one?
- Where is the longest shot or pause, and does it earn its length?
- If there is a retention graph, take a frame at every drop (`--at`) and name what happens there.

**Payoff, drives likes, shares and rewatches.** Look at the last 20 percent.
- Is there a turn, a reveal, an answer to the opening?
- Would someone send this to a friend? Name who, and why.
- Does the ending loop back into the start?

**Words.** Read the on-screen text, the voice and the caption.
- Does the caption carry on the reel or repeat it?
- Is the call to action one clear thing, or none?

**Brand (branded reels only).** When does the brand or product appear, and does the idea hold up without it?

**Comments.** Read what people actually said. Comments that name a moment ("the dog!") confirm a cause. Comments that tag a friend are public sends: count them. Comments that are only emojis say the reel was liked but did not give people anything to say.

For every cause, write: the moment (timestamp), what is on screen, the signal it moved, and a confidence. High: the numbers and the frames agree, and there is no other explanation. Medium: it fits, but another explanation is possible. Low: a guess worth testing.

## 4. Leak and lever

Choose one of each. The leak is the thing that cost the reel the most. The lever is the thing that carried it the furthest. A flop still has a lever and a winner still has a leak.

Both follow the same standard as a cause: a timestamp, a confidence, and nothing you did not see and hear. A stretch you could not check (a black screen whose sound you did not hear, a part with no frames) can never be the leak. If the evidence for a leak is thin, say "no clear leak" and name the weakest moment with a low confidence.

## 5. Lessons

Split them:

- **Carries over:** rules that would work for this account (or the user's) next time. Write each as an instruction: "Put the subject in the first frame", not "The subject came late".
- **Does not carry over:** what came from budget, a celebrity, a trend, a boost or luck. Copying these will not repeat the result.

## 6. Next test

One concrete experiment for the next reel: change one thing, keep the rest, and name the number that will show whether it worked.

## 7. What would sharpen this

List only the missing data that would change the diagnosis, and why. For someone else's reel this is usually the retention graph and the share count.

## Comparing two reels

When the user gives two reels from the same account, diagnose each, then add what the better one did that the weaker one did not. That contrast is often the clearest lesson of all.
